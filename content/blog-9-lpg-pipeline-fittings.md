# LPG Pipeline Fittings Checklist: A Practical Guide from Trench to Commissioning

Say a new LPG line at a restaurant is finished on schedule. The installer packs up, the gas is switched on, and the kitchen runs dinner service that same night. Three weeks later, the manager notices a faint smell near the back wall. Nobody can find the source. After two days of searching, the culprit turns up: one coupling, tightened to full torque in a single go, in about thirty seconds.

In most LPG leaks, the pipe and the material are fine. The problem is a small step someone skipped because the site was running late, like thread tape on a joint that should never have it, a trench bedded on leftover bricks, or a one-hour pressure test cut down to fifteen minutes.

With LPG, a small leak doesn't stay small. The gas is heavier than air, so it sinks into trenches, drains and basements and sits there until something ignites it.

This checklist walks you through every stage of an LPG pipeline installation, from digging the trench to signing off the final tightness test. It's written for site engineers, installers, facility managers and anyone who has to put their name on a commissioning report. Print it, keep it on site, and don't sign anything until every box is ticked.

---

## Quick summary

* **Bury the pipe at least 600 mm deep**, on and around clean sand. No bricks, wood or stones under the pipe.
* **Never use sealant or tape on conical metal-to-metal fittings.** Thread tape goes on NPT threads only.
* **Tighten coupling bolts in three stages** (20 Nm, 40 Nm, 47 Nm) with rest periods in between.
* **Purge with nitrogen** if the line has ever held fuel or vapour. Never use compressed air for this.
* **Run two tests before commissioning:** a 25 bar strength test for 5 minutes and a low-pressure tightness test for 1 hour.
* **Never tighten anything while the system is under pressure.**

## Introduction and compliance scope

### What this checklist covers

This is a step-by-step audit for LPG pipeline fittings, pipe joints and pre-commissioning safety on commercial kitchens, industrial plants and fuelling stations. You can use it during installation or as a final sign-off before the system goes live. Most of these checks also apply if you run a [domestic LPG gas pipeline service](/services/domestic-lpg-pipeline) for apartments and gated communities, just at a smaller scale.

#### Standards it draws from

* OPW KPS installation guidelines for underground flexible piping
* INGAA guidelines on pipeline pressure testing
* Bureau of Indian Standards (IS 8737 and IS 3224) for valve fittings
* Your local regulatory and fire safety approval requirements

Always check the manufacturer's manual for the exact pipe and fittings you're using. Where it differs from a general guideline, the manufacturer's numbers usually win for warranty purposes.

#### Why more sites are switching to pipelines

If you or your client are still weighing [pipeline vs cylinder](/blog/domestic-lpg-pipeline-vs-cylinder), the difference shows up fast in a busy kitchen or factory. Cylinders need constant swapping, storage space and someone keeping track of refills. They also struggle with heavy, continuous use. Draw gas out fast enough and you get [LPG cylinder freezing](/blog/lpg-gas-freezing): the cylinder chills, frost forms on the outside and the flame drops right when demand is highest. A pipeline fed from a central cylinder bank or bulk tank avoids most of that, as long as the installation is done right.

#### Why LPG needs extra care

Three properties of LPG explain why the rules below are so strict. One litre of liquid LPG expands into roughly 250 litres of gas, so a small liquid leak turns into a large vapour cloud quickly. The gas is heavier than air, so instead of drifting away it collects in trenches, basements, drains and pits. And it ignites at fairly low concentrations, roughly 2% to 10% of the air. Below that range the mix is too lean to burn and above it too rich, but in an enclosed space that window is easy to hit.

## Pre-installation and trench readiness checklist

Most fitting problems that show up years later start with a badly prepared trench. Pipe that shifts or sits on a hard point puts stress on the joints, and joints are where leaks begin.

* **Burial depth is at least 600 mm (60 cm)** from the top of the pipe to finished ground level.
* **The trench floor has at least 200 mm (20 cm) of clean sand**, with a grain size of about 3 mm.
* **There is 200 mm of sand on all sides of the pipe.**
* **No wood blocks, bricks, stones or Styrofoam sit under or around the pipe.** These create pressure points that wear the pipe and stress fittings over time.
* **If the pipe needs lifting during laying**, sandbags are placed at 1-metre intervals.
* **Backfill is compacted mechanically** every 20 cm.
* **At least 30 cm of compacted sand covers the pipe** before any heavy vehicle or equipment crosses the trench.
* **Marker tape is laid above the pipe** during backfilling so anyone digging later knows what's below.

Before backfilling, take photos of the open trench with a measuring tape in frame. Once the sand goes in, nobody can check the depth again without digging it up.

## Fitting assembly and jointing checklist

This is where most installers get caught out, because NPT threads and conical seals need opposite treatment. Tape that seals a threaded joint will cause a conical joint to leak.

### NPT tapered threads

* Threads are free of damage, burrs and cross-threading before assembly.
* Gas-rated thread sealing tape is applied. Standard plumbing tape for water lines isn't rated for gas.

### Conical seals (tees, elbows, manifolds)

* Nothing has been applied to metal-to-metal conical seals: no lubricant, no thread tape, no sealant.

These fittings seal through direct metal contact. Anything between the two surfaces stops them seating properly, and you end up with a joint that looks tight and leaks under pressure.

### Coupling fasteners

* Coupling bolts and nuts are coated with copper paste, ceramic paste or molybdenum disulphide (MoS2) before torquing. This prevents galling and keeps torque readings accurate.

### Staged torque sequence

Couplings need time for the seal material to settle. Going straight to final torque in one go is a common reason joints loosen later.

* **Stage 1, 20 Nm:** tighten in alternate half-turns, rest 5 minutes, and repeat 3 times.
* **Stage 2, 40 Nm:** tighten to 40 Nm, rest 15 minutes, and repeat 2 times.
* **Stage 3, 47 Nm:** apply final torque and rest 15 to 30 minutes, rechecking until the coupling is fully closed and holds torque.
* Torque values and times for each coupling are recorded on the installation sheet.

Yes, this takes a while on a site with dozens of couplings. Build it into the schedule instead of trying to squeeze it.

## Safety, purging and exclusion zone checklist

### Inert gas flushing

* If the line has ever carried fuel or hydrocarbon vapour (during re-testing or repair, for example), it has been fully purged with nitrogen.
* Compressed air was never used to purge these lines. Air mixed with leftover fuel vapour can form an explosive mixture inside the pipe.

### Exclusion zones

* For hydrostatic testing, a perimeter of at least 50 feet (about 15 m) is set around test headers and pressurised sections.
* For pneumatic testing, a larger exclusion boundary is worked out from line volume, pipe diameter and test pressure. Gas stores far more energy than water at the same pressure, so a pneumatic failure is much more violent.
* Only essential, trained personnel are inside the zone during testing.

### Signage and barriers

* "DANGER: HIGH PRESSURE TESTING IN PROGRESS" signs are posted at every access point.
* Physical barriers or blast shielding are in place wherever people or equipment could be exposed.

#### Why pneumatic testing is riskier

Water barely compresses, so when a hydrostatic test fails, the pressure drops almost instantly and the water leaks out. Compressed gas behaves differently. When a joint fails under pneumatic pressure, the stored energy releases all at once and can throw fittings or pipe sections with real force. That's why pneumatic tests need wider exclusion zones and tighter controls.

## Pre-commissioning pressure and tightness testing checklist

The strength test checks that the system holds together under load. The tightness test finds the small leaks the strength test misses.

### High-pressure strength test

* The pipeline is isolated from storage tanks and dispensers.
* It is pressurised to 25 bar (363 psi), and never above 40 bar.
* Nitrogen is used. Air is acceptable only on brand-new lines that have never held fuel.
* Pressure is held for 5 minutes.
* The line is inspected for structural movement, displaced fittings and unanchored sections that shift.

### Low-pressure tightness test

Gas detectors earn their place once the system is running, but on a fresh installation the soap test is still the simplest and most reliable form of [LPG leak detection](/blog/lpg-leak-detection). It points you to the exact joint that's leaking, which a room detector can't do.

* Pressure is set between 0.02 and 0.70 bar (0.3 to 10 psi).
* Pressure is held for 1 hour.
* Soap solution (water plus detergent) is applied to every joint, fitting and flange.
* The undersides of pipes and fittings are checked with a mirror.
* No bubbles appear. Even slow, small bubbles mean a leak that needs fixing.

### Temperature and pressure logging

* Ambient temperature and gauge pressure are recorded every 10 minutes during the test.

Pressure in a sealed pipe rises and falls with temperature, so a line tested in the morning sun can show a pressure change with no leak at all. Use the gas law P1/T1 = P2/T2 (with absolute pressure and temperature in Kelvin) to check whether a drop matches the temperature change or points to a real leak.

### Strength test vs tightness test

| Criteria | Strength test | Tightness test |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Purpose** | Confirms the system can hold pressure without failing | Finds small leaks at joints and fittings |
| **Pressure** | 25 bar (max 40 bar) | 0.02 to 0.70 bar |
| **Hold time** | 5 minutes | 1 hour |
| **Test medium** | Nitrogen (air only on new, unused lines) | Nitrogen or air as per manufacturer |
| **What to check** | Pipe movement, fitting displacement, anchoring | Bubbles at joints, pressure drop vs temperature log |
| **Pass condition** | No movement or failure | No bubbles, no unexplained pressure drop |

## Emergency handling and maintenance

### If you smell gas or find a leak

1. Shut off the main gas supply immediately.
2. Open doors and windows for natural ventilation. Don't switch electrical equipment on or off.
3. Fully depressurise the system before anyone inspects or repairs it.

### Never tighten under pressure

Don't tighten fittings, flange bolts or couplings while the system is pressurised. It feels like a quick fix, but a fitting that gives way under load can release gas at full pressure right at the person holding the spanner. Depressurise first, every time.

## Conclusion

Nearly every step in this checklist exists because someone, somewhere, skipped it and had a leak. Dig the trench to depth, bed the pipe on sand, keep tape off conical fittings, give the couplings their rest periods, and run both tests for the full time. Each one is simple on its own; the risk comes from letting one slide when the site is behind schedule.

Keep signed copies of every completed checklist, torque record and test log. They protect you during inspections and warranty claims, and the next maintenance team will know exactly what was done.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can thread tape be used on conical fittings?
No. Conical fittings seal through direct metal-to-metal contact. Tape or sealant stops them seating properly, which leads to leaks. Use thread tape only on NPT tapered threads.

### Why is nitrogen needed when re-testing pipes that have carried fuel?
Leftover fuel vapour mixed with air inside the pipe can form an explosive mixture. Nitrogen is inert, so it pushes the vapour out without adding oxygen.

### What documents do I need for warranty validation?
Usually signed installation checklists, conductivity logs and 60-minute tightness test records showing pressure over time. Check your manufacturer's warranty terms for anything extra.

### How deep should an underground LPG pipe be buried?
At least 600 mm from the top of the pipe to finished ground level, with 200 mm of sand below and around it.

### Why can't I use bricks or wood to support the pipe in the trench?
They create hard pressure points. Over time, ground movement and vibration press the pipe against them, which can damage the pipe wall and stress nearby fittings.

### My pressure dropped during the tightness test. Does that mean there's a leak?
Not always. Check your temperature log first. If the temperature fell, pressure will fall with it. Use P1/T1 = P2/T2 to see whether the drop matches. If it doesn't, or if you see bubbles at any joint, you have a leak.
