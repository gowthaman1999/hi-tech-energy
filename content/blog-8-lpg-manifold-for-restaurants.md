# LPG Manifold for Restaurant: How to Stop Running Out of Gas in the Middle of Service

Ask a restaurant owner about the worst ten minutes of their year, and there's a good chance a gas cylinder is part of the story.

It's 8:40 on a Saturday. Every table is taken, the rail has 19 tickets on it, and your butter chicken is the best it's been all month. Then the flame under it shrinks, coughs, and goes out. Two burners down the line follow. Your helper is dragging a 19 kg cylinder across a wet floor while the head chef shouts for a spanner, and out front a family of six is checking their watches.

By the time the gas is back, you've remade four dishes, sent out free desserts, and you already know which table is going to leave the one-star review.

What makes this so frustrating is that you could have seen it coming. The number of burners you run, the size of your cylinders, and the hours you're busiest were all there to work out on paper months ago. The kitchen simply outgrew a gas setup built for half the covers.

An LPG manifold is how busy restaurants stop this from happening. It links several cylinders into one supply, keeps a full backup bank ready, and switches to it by itself the moment the first bank runs low. Your cooks keep cooking, and the guests never find out there was a problem.

This guide covers everything you need before you buy or upgrade one: how the system works, how to size it for your kitchen with a real worked example, the safety and PESO rules you have to meet, and the small daily habits that keep it running without surprises.

---

## Key Takeaways

The short version:

* **A manifold removes mid-service gas outages.** One bank of cylinders feeds the kitchen while a second bank waits. When the first runs low, an automatic changeover regulator switches to the reserve without any flame drop.
* **Size it on real demand, not guesswork.** Add up the gas rating of every appliance, multiply by 0.7, and check that your active cylinders can vaporise gas fast enough to keep up.
* **Cylinder freezing is the hidden problem.** Pull gas faster than a cylinder can boil it off and you get frost on the cylinder, a pressure crash, and flames going out. More active cylinders solve this.
* **Cylinders belong outdoors.** LPG vapour is heavier than air and pools at floor level. Basements and indoor storerooms are off the table.
* **Safety devices are not optional.** Leak sensors, a solenoid shut-off tied to your exhaust hood, excess flow valves and flame failure devices all need to be in place and tested.
* **Most restaurants land on a 2×2 or 4×4 manifold.** High-volume and cloud kitchens often need 6×6, 8×8 or a vaporiser setup.

### The Hidden Cost of Mid-Service Gas Depletion

Most small restaurants start the same way. A couple of commercial cylinders, a regulator on each, a rubber hose to the range. It works fine at 30 covers a night, and then the business grows and it stops working.

A single cylinder setup has no warning and no backup. When the cylinder empties, the flame dies, usually right when demand is highest because that's when you're burning gas fastest. Your line loses momentum, half-cooked dishes have to be redone, and someone ends up handling a heavy cylinder in a rush. Rushed cylinder swaps are where leaks and loose fittings happen.

Connecting cylinders one-to-one with appliances doesn't hold up in a busy kitchen for two reasons. First, each cylinder empties fast because one appliance is pulling everything from it. Second, as a cylinder empties and cools, its pressure drops, and your flame gets weaker even before it runs out. Your chef notices the tadka isn't crackling the way it should, but nobody knows why.

A central gas system with a manifold fixes both problems. Gas comes from a common header fed by several cylinders, so pressure stays steady. Cooking temperatures stay consistent, burners don't get starved of gas, and your equipment lasts longer because it isn't constantly running on low or fluctuating pressure. For most restaurants, the [LPG pipeline vs cylinder](/blog/domestic-lpg-pipeline-vs-cylinder) question comes down to exactly this: loose cylinders cost you a little in every service, and a piped system stops that cost.

#### What 15 minutes of downtime really costs

Say your restaurant serves 40 covers an hour at peak with an average bill of ₹600. A 15-minute gas outage during dinner rush can easily mean:

* Around 10 covers delayed or lost: ₹6,000
* Dishes remade or comped to calm angry tables: ₹1,500 to ₹3,000
* One bad Google review that sits at the top of your listing for months: hard to put a number on

Do that twice a month and you've paid for a basic manifold within a year.

## What is a Restaurant LPG Manifold System?

An LPG manifold is a piping assembly that connects multiple LPG cylinders into one common supply line, called a header. Instead of each appliance depending on its own cylinder, the whole kitchen draws from the header.

The main parts you'll find in a typical system:

* **Active cylinder bank:** the group of cylinders currently feeding the kitchen
* **Reserve cylinder bank:** an equal group sitting on standby
* **Automatic changeover regulator:** the valve that switches from active to reserve when the active side runs low
* **First-stage and second-stage regulators:** these bring the gas pressure down in two steps to what your appliances need
* **High-pressure pigtails:** short flexible hoses connecting each cylinder to the header, usually stainless steel braided
* **Isolation and emergency shut-off valves:** for swapping cylinders safely and cutting gas in an emergency

The whole point is to give your ranges, tandoors, fryers and ovens a steady, uninterrupted gas supply at the right pressure, while moving the high-pressure cylinders out of the kitchen to a safer spot outdoors.

## Core System Architecture & How It Works

### Active vs. Reserve Cylinder Banks & Auto-Changeover Regulators

A manifold splits the cylinders into two equal groups. You'll see this written as 2×2, 4×4 or 6×6. A 4×4, for example, has 8 cylinders: 4 on the active side and 4 on the reserve side.

The active bank feeds the kitchen. The reserve bank sits there full, doing nothing, until it's needed.

The automatic changeover regulator sits between the two banks. It's constantly sensing pressure on the active side. As those cylinders empty, the pressure falls. When it drops below a set point, a spring-loaded mechanism inside the regulator shifts and opens the reserve side.

There's no electricity involved, no staff action and no gap in supply. The burners keep going at the same flame.

Most changeover regulators have a coloured indicator:

* **Green** means the active bank is still supplying gas. All good.
* **Red** means the active bank is empty and the system has switched to reserve.

When your staff see red, that's the cue to call your gas distributor and order a refill for the empty bank. You now have the full reserve bank's worth of time to get it done, which for most restaurants is a day or more.

Add one line to your opening checklist: whoever opens the kitchen checks the indicator window, and if it's red, the refill call happens before 11 am. Kitchens that skip this end up with both banks empty on a Sunday, which is the exact problem the manifold was bought to fix.

### Two-Stage Pressure Regulation (High to Medium to Working Pressure)

Gas inside a cylinder is under a lot of pressure. Your burners need very little. Getting from one to the other in a single step works on a home stove, but in a commercial kitchen with long pipe runs and lots of appliances it causes problems. That's why manifolds use two stages.

**First stage: at the manifold.** The pressure inside an LPG cylinder changes with temperature. On a mild day it might be around 4 to 8 bar, and in peak Indian summer it climbs higher. Regulators at this point are built to handle up to roughly 15 to 17.5 bar. The first-stage regulator brings this down to a medium pressure, usually around 1.4 to 2 bar. This medium pressure is what travels through the pipeline toward the kitchen.

**Second stage: near the appliances.** Close to the kitchen, a second regulator drops the pressure to what each appliance is designed for. Standard low-pressure appliances run at roughly 28 to 50 mbar (about 11 inches water column). Some Indian commercial burners, like Chinese wok ranges and bulk-cooking bhattis, are designed for higher pressure, so the second stage is set to match those specifically.

Two stages matter most when demand jumps. At lunch, the tandoor is already going, three burners on the range light up together, and the fryers kick in. With a single regulator, that sudden demand causes the pressure to dip, and you get lifting flames, yellow tips, or in bad cases flashback inside the burner. With two stages, the medium-pressure line acts as a buffer. The second-stage regulator has plenty of supply to draw on, so pressure at the burner stays steady even when everything lights at once.

| Feature / Specification | First-Stage Regulator | Second-Stage Regulator |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Input pressure** | Cylinder pressure (up to 15 to 17.5 bar rated) | Around 1.4 to 2 bar |
| **Output pressure** | Around 1.4 to 2 bar | 28 to 50 mbar for low-pressure appliances, or higher as specified |
| **Location** | At the manifold header, outdoors | Near the kitchen wall or at the appliance branch |
| **Main purpose** | Bring raw cylinder pressure to a safe, steady medium pressure for the pipeline | Deliver exact working pressure for clean, stable flames |

### High-Pressure Pigtails, Manifold Headers & Safety Valves

**Pigtails.** These are the short flexible hoses connecting each cylinder valve to the header. You'll find stainless steel braided pigtails (preferred for commercial use) or reinforced rubber ones. Good pigtails have a built-in check valve, so if one hose is disconnected or damaged, gas from the header doesn't flow back out through it.

**Isolation valves and non-return valves (NRVs).** Each cylinder branch should have its own shut-off valve. This is what lets your staff close one cylinder, remove it, and fit a new one while the rest of the system keeps feeding the kitchen. Non-return valves stop gas from flowing backwards from a full cylinder into an empty one or out of an open connection.

## Right-Sizing Your Restaurant LPG Manifold

Most restaurant owners skip this part, and most manifold problems start here. An undersized manifold still fails at peak hour, only more slowly than a single cylinder does. Sizing one properly takes three steps.

### Step 1: Calculate Total Peak Hourly Gas Consumption

List every gas appliance. Walk the kitchen and note the gas rating on each appliance. It's usually on a metal nameplate on the side or back, given in BTU/hr, kW, or kg/hr.

As a rough rule, 1 kg of LPG gives about 47,000 BTU of heat. So to turn BTU/hr into kg/hr, divide by 47,000.

Here's a sample kitchen for a busy North Indian restaurant:

| Appliance | Rating (BTU/hr) | Approx. LPG use (kg/hr) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Commercial 6-burner range | 108,000 | 2.30 |
| Heavy-duty tandoor | 40,000 | 0.85 |
| Twin deep fryers | 60,000 | 1.28 |
| Stock pot burner | 50,000 | 1.06 |
| Flat-top griddle | 30,000 | 0.64 |
| **Total connected load** | **288,000** | **6.13** |

Total connected load is simply all of these added together. It's the most gas your kitchen could ever use if every burner was on full at the same time.

### Step 2: Apply the Diversity Factor (0.7)

No kitchen runs every burner at full flame at once. Even at peak, some burners are on simmer, the fryer is idling between batches, and the griddle is half-used. Planning for 100% would mean paying for a much bigger system than you need.

To account for this, multiply your total connected load by 0.7 to get a realistic peak demand. It's a widely used benchmark for commercial kitchens.

Worked example using the kitchen above:

* **Total connected load:** 288,000 BTU/hr (6.13 kg/hr)
* **Diversity factor:** 0.7
* **Realistic peak demand:** 288,000 × 0.7 = 201,600 BTU/hr
* **In kg:** 201,600 ÷ 47,000 = about 4.3 kg/hr

So this kitchen needs a system that can deliver around 4.3 kg of LPG every hour during peak service, without pressure dropping.

If your kitchen is unusual, say a banquet kitchen where every burner really does run flat out for three hours, use a higher factor like 0.85 or 0.9.

### Step 3: Factor in Cylinder Evaporation Capacity (Preventing Freeze)

Inside the cylinder, LPG sits as a liquid. To come out as gas, it has to boil, and boiling takes heat. That heat comes from the air around the cylinder, through the steel wall. As gas is drawn off, the cylinder gets colder.

If you pull gas faster than the cylinder can absorb heat, it keeps getting colder. You'll see condensation on the outside, then a ring of frost at the liquid level. Pressure inside drops sharply. Your flames go weak and can go out completely, even though the cylinder is still half full. This is one of the most common reasons for "the gas is low but the cylinder isn't empty" complaints.

Each cylinder can only safely supply so much gas per hour. That depends on its size (bigger cylinders have more wetted surface area to absorb heat), how full it is, and the outside temperature. Rough figures in typical Indian conditions:

* **19 kg commercial cylinder:** around 0.8 to 1 kg/hr of continuous draw
* **47.5 kg cylinder:** around 1.5 to 2 kg/hr of continuous draw

These drop on cold mornings, in hill stations, or when cylinders are kept in shade with poor airflow. Your gas supplier or installer can give you exact figures for your cylinder type.

Going back to the example, the kitchen needs 4.3 kg/hr. Using 47.5 kg cylinders at about 1.5 kg/hr each:

* 4.3 ÷ 1.5 = 2.9, so at least 3 cylinders on the active side
* Round up for safety and you get 4 active cylinders
* Match that with 4 reserve cylinders, and you have a 4×4 manifold

If this kitchen used 19 kg cylinders instead, you'd need about 5 to 6 active cylinders, which pushes you to a 6×6.

## Manifold Selection Matrix by Restaurant Type

Here's a quick guide. It assumes 47.5 kg cylinders in normal Indian ambient temperatures. If you're on 19 kg cylinders, go one size up.

| Restaurant type | Typical equipment | Peak demand | Recommended setup |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Café, QSR, small bistro** | 2 to 4 burner range, 1 fryer, small griddle | Up to 3 kg/hr | 2×2 manifold (4 cylinders) |
| **Casual dining, busy bar kitchen** | 6-burner range, tandoor, twin fryers, stock pot | 3 to 8 kg/hr | 4×4 manifold (8 cylinders) |
| **High-volume dining, banquets, hotel kitchens** | Multiple ranges, Chinese wok ranges, bulk bhattis, ovens | 8 to 15 kg/hr | 6×6 manifold (12 cylinders) |
| **Cloud kitchens, central production units** | Several cooking lines running long hours | 15+ kg/hr | 8×8 manifold or LOT with vaporiser |

Treat this as a starting point. Always run the three-step calculation for your actual kitchen.

The pipes matter too. A well-sized manifold won't help if the pipeline after it is too narrow. [Gas pipe sizing for LPG](/blog/lpg-pipe-sizing) depends on your peak demand in kg/hr, the length of the run from the manifold to the farthest appliance, and the pressure in that section of pipe. A long, thin pipe running at low pressure loses too much on the way, and the burner at the far end of the line ends up with a weak flame. Your installer should work out the pipe diameter from these figures. If they just pick a standard size without asking about your appliances, find someone else.

## Safety Infrastructure, Storage & Statutory Compliance

A manifold makes your kitchen safer, but only if it's installed right. This section covers what the rules expect and why.

In India, LPG storage and installations come under the Gas Cylinder Rules administered by PESO (Petroleum and Explosives Safety Organisation), along with IS 6044 Part 2 for commercial LPG installations and your state fire service's NOC requirements. Depending on how much LPG you store on site, you may need a PESO licence. Internationally, NFPA 58 is the common reference. Your installer should know which apply to you.

### Outdoor Storage & Heavy-Vapour Clearance Rules

LPG vapour is about 1.5 to 2 times heavier than air. Unlike natural gas, which rises and disperses, leaked LPG flows downward and collects at floor level, in drains, pits, and any low corner. It can sit there quietly until it finds a spark.

The cylinder bank belongs outdoors, on a level concrete base, inside a lockable enclosure made of wire mesh or with louvred sides. The enclosure needs airflow on all sides so any small leak blows away instead of building up. A roof to protect from direct sun and rain is good. Solid walls on all sides are not.

Keep the cylinder bank well clear of:

* Doors and windows, especially ones that open into the kitchen
* Basement openings and stairwells
* Air intakes for AC or ventilation
* Electrical switches, panels, and motors
* Open drains and manholes

Typical minimum distances range from 1 to 3 metres depending on the quantity stored and local codes.

Cylinders can never go in basements, cellars, lofts, attics, under staircases, inside the main kitchen, or anywhere along an escape route. Fire codes in India and pretty much everywhere else prohibit this, because a leak in an enclosed low space turns into an explosion risk very quickly.

### Gas Safety Interlocks & Forced Ventilation

A commercial kitchen's gas supply should be electrically linked to the exhaust hood fans. If the hood isn't running, the gas shouldn't flow.

In practice, a solenoid valve sits on the main gas line into the kitchen. It only stays open while the exhaust system is on and an airflow sensor confirms air is actually moving. If the fan trips, a belt snaps, or someone switches the hood off mid-service, the solenoid closes and gas stops.

Burning gas without proper extraction fills the kitchen with carbon monoxide, which you can't see or smell. It also allows unburned gas to build up if a burner goes out. Your cooks are standing in that air for 10 hours a day.

### Automatic Safety Shut-Offs & Leak Detection

**Excess flow valves (EFVs).** These valves close automatically if gas starts flowing much faster than the system is designed for. That only happens when something has gone badly wrong, like a pipe cracking or a pigtail getting cut. The EFV limits how much gas escapes.

**Gas leak detectors.** Good [LPG leak detection](/blog/lpg-leak-detection) depends mostly on where the sensors go. Since LPG settles low, sensors should be mounted about 30 cm above floor level near the appliances and the point where gas enters the kitchen. Good systems link the sensors to a loud alarm and to the solenoid valve, so a detected leak shuts off the gas automatically.

**Flame failure devices (FFDs).** Every burner should have one. A thermocouple sits in the flame. If the flame blows out from a draft, a boil-over, or a cook's mistake, the thermocouple cools down within seconds and cuts gas to that burner. Without it, an unlit burner keeps pouring gas into the kitchen.

### Piping Colour Coding, Flexible Hoses & Equipment Restraints

Fixed gas pipelines should be painted signal red or yellow, with "LPG" and flow direction arrows stencilled at regular intervals. When a plumber, electrician, or fire officer walks into your kitchen, they should know straight away which line is gas.

**Flexible hoses:**

* Keep the hose from the fixed pipe to the appliance as short as practical, usually no more than 1.2 to 2 metres depending on local rules.
* Stainless steel braided hoses hold up far better to heat, grease, and rats than rubber.
* Rubber hoses should be replaced every 2 years, or sooner if you see cracks, hardening, or swelling.

Ranges and fryers on castors get pulled out for cleaning. Without a restraint, the only thing stopping them is the gas hose, which gets stretched and kinked until it fails. A steel safety cable anchored to the wall, shorter than the gas hose, takes that load instead.

## Operational Best Practices, Inspections & Troubleshooting

A well-installed manifold still needs a bit of daily care. Most of it takes minutes.

### Safe Cylinder Replacement & Soap-Bubble Leak Checks

Changeover routine for staff:

1. Check the indicator window. Red means the bank that just emptied needs replacing.
2. Close the cylinder valves on the empty cylinders.
3. Close the isolation valve on each empty branch.
4. Disconnect the pigtails carefully. A small hiss of trapped gas is normal.
5. Check the cylinder valve threads and the pigtail washer or O-ring. Replace any worn washers.
6. Fit the new cylinders and tighten the connections firmly, without over-tightening.
7. Open the cylinder valves slowly, then open the branch isolation valves.
8. Turn the changeover lever so the newly filled bank becomes the reserve.

**The soap-bubble test.** After every cylinder change, brush soapy water or a proper leak detection fluid onto each joint you touched. Watch for 10 to 15 seconds. Any bubbles growing means a leak: close the valve and re-tighten or replace the washer.

Never check for leaks with a match or lighter. Everyone knows this, and people still do it.

### Daily Kitchen Shift Shutdown Sequence

At the end of the night, do it in this order:

1. Close the main manifold shut-off valve first, while the burners are still lit.
2. Let the gas left in the pipeline burn off. The flames will shrink and go out on their own.
3. Then turn every appliance knob to OFF.

Doing it in this order empties the pipeline, so if a hose fails or someone bumps a knob overnight, there's no pressurised gas sitting in the kitchen. Turning the knobs off first and the main valve second leaves the whole line full.

### Mid-Service Emergency Troubleshooting Guide

#### Problem 1: Flames dropping or flickering during peak hours

Likely causes:
* Active cylinders freezing (look for frost or heavy condensation)
* Changeover regulator stuck and not switching to reserve
* Manifold too small for the current load

What staff should do right away:
* Check cylinders for frost. If frozen, manually switch to the reserve bank.
* Check the indicator. If it's red but flames are still low, the changeover may be stuck. Switch manually.
* Reduce load by turning off burners not in active use until pressure recovers.
* Afterwards, get the system re-checked. If this keeps happening, you've outgrown your manifold.

*Don't pour hot water on frozen cylinders or try to heat them. Report it and get the system resized.*

#### Problem 2: Gas keeps shutting off for no clear reason

This is usually the safety interlock doing its job, or a sensor that needs attention.
* Check the exhaust hood is actually running at full speed.
* Look for blocked filters or a slipping fan belt reducing airflow.
* Leak sensors can drift or get coated in grease. Have them cleaned and recalibrated by your installer.

*Never bypass or bridge the interlock to "get through service." Of all the shortcuts kitchens take, this is one of the most dangerous.*

#### Problem 3: You smell gas

LPG has no smell on its own. The rotten-egg or cabbage smell is a chemical called ethyl mercaptan added so you can detect leaks. If you smell it:

1. Don't touch any electrical switch: no lights, no fans, no phones inside the kitchen.
2. Put out all open flames.
3. Close the main manifold valve and the cylinder valves if you can reach them safely.
4. Open doors and windows, especially low ones, to let the gas flow out.
5. Get everyone out of the building.
6. Call your gas supplier's emergency line and the fire service from outside.
7. Don't go back in until a qualified person confirms it's safe.

## Conclusion

When a kitchen runs out of gas mid-service, it usually means the gas setup hasn't kept up with how much the business has grown.

A properly designed LPG manifold gives you steady gas through the busiest hours, a full reserve that switches in by itself, and cylinders stored safely outside the kitchen where they belong. Add the right safety devices, and you get a kitchen that's easier to run, easier to get through a fire audit, and a lot less stressful on a packed Saturday.

Most of this comes down to sizing. Count your appliances, apply the 0.7 factor, check your cylinders can keep up without freezing, and pick the configuration that matches.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

### How long do flexible pigtails and braided hoses last in a commercial kitchen?
Rubber pigtails and hoses should be checked every month and replaced every 2 years, even if they look fine. Rubber hardens and cracks from the inside. Stainless steel braided pigtails can last up to 5 years, but your local rules (PESO, NFPA, or your fire authority) have the final say, so follow whichever is stricter.

### Can LPG cylinders be stored inside a restaurant or basement?
No. Because LPG vapour is heavier than air, a leak in a basement or closed indoor room collects at floor level and can't escape. That's why it's prohibited almost everywhere. Manifold cylinder banks need to be outdoors, at ground level, in a well-ventilated enclosure.

### What's the difference between Vapour Off-Take (VOT) and Liquid Off-Take (LOT) manifolds?
A VOT system draws gas vapour from the top of the cylinders and depends on the cylinders boiling LPG naturally using heat from the surrounding air. It works well for small and mid-sized restaurants using up to about 10 to 15 kg/hr.

An LOT system draws liquid LPG from the bottom of special cylinders and sends it through a vaporiser, which uses electric or hot water heat to turn it into gas. Because it doesn't rely on the cylinders to do the boiling, there's no freezing problem. It's the usual choice for large hotels, central kitchens and cloud kitchens using 15 to 100+ kg/hr.

### When should a restaurant switch from a cylinder manifold to a bulk LPG tank?
Once you're using more than about 1,500 to 2,000 kg of LPG a month, or your continuous demand goes above roughly 25 kg/hr, juggling dozens of cylinders starts to cost you in labour, space and delivery hassle. At that point, a bulk tank outdoors with scheduled tanker refills usually works out cheaper per kg and much easier to manage.

### How often should a restaurant LPG manifold be serviced?
Do a quick visual check and soap-bubble test after every cylinder change. Have a certified gas engineer inspect the full system, including regulators, pressure settings, safety valves, interlocks and leak detectors, at least once a year. Busy kitchens running long hours benefit from a check every six months.

### Can I add more appliances to my existing manifold later?
Sometimes. If your current setup has spare capacity, adding a burner or two is usually fine. But every new appliance adds to your peak demand. Redo the sizing calculation first. If the new total is close to what your active bank can vaporise, plan to move up a size rather than risk freezing and pressure drops during service.
