## Quick summary
* Most gas line problems are sizing problems. Lazy flames, sooty burners and a water heater that cuts out when the furnace kicks on usually trace back to a pipe that's one size too small.
* LPG carries about 2.5 times more heat per cubic foot than natural gas, so the same load needs a smaller LPG line. Never size an LPG job off a natural gas table.
* Every capacity table is built on a set pressure drop, and it's printed in the header. Mixing a 0.5" w.c. table with a 1.0 psi table is one of the most common ways good fitters end up with undersized runs.
* The longest length method gives you a fast, safe answer. The branch length method stops you paying for oversized pipe on short branches.
* On LPG, check the supply before blaming the pipe. With vapour off-take, the cylinders can only boil off gas so fast, which is why the way you draw gas matters on bigger loads. A frosted cylinder points to the supply, not a sizing fault.
* A flat 5 feet per fitting overstates losses on small pipe and badly understates them on large headers.
* Bond to rigid pipe or a brass fitting. A clamp on the corrugated CSST itself is a code violation.
* Test, purge and check regulator lock-up before you hand over the job. A tightness test done with the wrong gauge proves nothing.

Most of us have taken this call. The customer installed a new tankless water heater, and it works fine on its own. But turn on the shower while the furnace is running and the heater throws an ignition fault. The installer says the unit is faulty and the manufacturer says it's the gas supply. Nine times out of ten, the manufacturer is right.

On paper, gas pipe sizing is simple: add up the BTUs, measure the run, read a table. In practice it's where small shortcuts turn into callbacks, and sometimes into real safety problems. I've written this guide for gas fitters, plumbers, system designers and teams running an [LPG gas pipeline installation service](https://www.hitechenergy.org/) who want a reference they can use on a job. It covers both LPG and natural gas.

### Why small sizing errors cause big field failures
Gas loses pressure to friction as it moves through a pipe. The longer the pipe, the smaller the bore and the more gas you push through it, the more pressure you lose. An undersized run starves the appliance of pressure, especially when several appliances fire at once. The burner then runs on the wrong gas and air mix, which gives you yellow, lazy flames and a carbon monoxide risk. Soot builds up on heat exchangers and burner heads and makes things worse over time. And you get nuisance flame-outs at peak demand: the system works perfectly at 2 p.m. and fails at 7 a.m., when everyone is showering and the heating is on.

The awkward part is that an undersized system often passes commissioning. You light one appliance at a time, everything looks fine, and the problems only show up weeks later under full load.

## Natural gas vs. LPG: why one table won't do both
The two fuels look the same on a burner but move through pipe very differently.

Natural gas has a specific gravity of about 0.60, so it's lighter than air, and it carries roughly 1,000 BTU per cubic foot. LPG (propane) has a specific gravity of about 1.50 to 1.52, so it's heavier than air and collects in basements, pits and drains. It carries roughly 2,500 BTU per cubic foot. Stored as a liquid, it expands about 250 times when it turns to vapour, which is how a modest cylinder or tank can feed a whole building.

Because each cubic foot of LPG holds so much more energy, you move less volume for the same heat load. An LPG line can often be one size smaller than a natural gas line serving the same appliances.

| Property | Natural gas | LPG (propane) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Specific gravity (air = 1.0)** | ~0.60 (lighter than air) | ~1.50 to 1.52 (heavier than air) |
| **Heating value** | ~1,000 BTU/ft³ | ~2,500 BTU/ft³ |
| **Liquid to vapour expansion** | Not applicable (delivered as gas) | ~250 times |
| **Typical appliance supply pressure** | 3.5" to 7" w.c. | 11" w.c. (~300 mm w.c.) |
| **Where leaks collect** | Ceilings and high points | Floors, pits and drains |

## Domestic LPG pipeline vs LPG cylinder: what changes for sizing
Plenty of homes still run a single cylinder with a rubber hose straight to the stove. There's no pipe to size, so nobody thinks about pressure drop.

That changes when a household moves to a piped system. The piped setup keeps the cylinders outside in a ventilated enclosure and runs fixed pipe to the kitchen, the geyser and anything else that burns gas. That's safer, and it frees up kitchen space. It also means you now have pipe length, fittings and a combined load to deal with, so the sizing rules in this guide apply.

A good [domestic LPG gas pipeline service](https://www.hitechenergy.org/services/domestic-lpg-pipeline) treats even a small flat like a small network. List the appliances, measure the run from the regulator, and size it properly.

## Operating pressures and allowable pressure drops
Before you open a capacity table, you need two numbers: the pressure at the start of the run, and how much of it you can lose before the gas reaches the appliance.

### How two-stage LPG and natural gas pressures work
Two-stage regulation is standard on most modern LPG installations. The first stage regulator sits at or near the tank or cylinder bank. Tank pressure swings a lot with outdoor temperature, and the first stage brings it down to a steady intermediate pressure of no more than 20 psig (about 1.4 bar). Many systems run around 10 psig. The second stage regulator sits closer to the building and drops that to appliance level, typically 11" w.c. (about 300 mm w.c., or roughly 27 to 29 mbar).

The long run from tank to building carries gas at higher pressure, so it can be smaller pipe. The second stage then gives the appliances a steady pressure even as tank pressure rises and falls.

### LOT vs VOT: how the supply source affects your system
On LPG jobs the pipe is only half the story. The other half is how the gas leaves the cylinders.

VOT (vapour off-take) draws vapour from the top of the cylinder, and the liquid inside boils off to replace it using heat from the surrounding air. It needs no extra equipment, which is why homes, small restaurants and offices use it.

LOT (liquid off-take) draws liquid LPG from the bottom of the cylinder and sends it to a vaporiser, which turns it into gas with electric or water-bath heat. Hotels, industrial kitchens and factories use it when the load is too high for natural boil-off.

For sizing, three things follow. VOT capacity is capped by vaporisation rate, so a perfect pipe still starves the appliances if the cylinder bank can't make enough vapour; add cylinders to the manifold, not bigger pipe. On an LOT system, the section up to the vaporiser carries liquid and is designed as a liquid line, including hydrostatic relief valves anywhere liquid could get trapped between two valves. After the vaporiser, you size it like any other vapour system, using the pressures and drops below.

[LPG cylinder freezing](https://www.hitechenergy.org/blog/lpg-gas-freezing) is the problem that passes for a sizing fault. When a VOT system draws gas faster than the cylinders can boil it off, the liquid cools. Frost forms on the outside of the cylinder, usually up to the liquid level. Cylinder pressure falls, flames shrink and appliances cut out, just as they would with an undersized pipe.

You can tell the two apart on site. If the cylinders are frosted or sweating heavily during peak use, the supply is the problem, not the pipe. Add cylinders to the manifold, switch to an LOT system with a vaporiser, or spread the load across more of the day. Never heat a cylinder with a flame or hot water to thaw it.

### Natural gas supply pressures
Natural gas usually arrives at the meter at low pressure, around 3.5" to 7" w.c. (about 0.125 to 0.25 psi) for most homes and small commercial sites. Larger buildings may get medium-pressure supply (2 psi or more) with line regulators near each appliance group, which lets you run smaller pipe for the mains.

## Choosing the correct allowable pressure drop
Every capacity table assumes a pressure drop. Common baselines are 0.5" w.c. for low-pressure natural gas and 11" w.c. LPG in US-style tables, 1.0 psi for 2 psi elevated-pressure systems, 1 to 2 mbar in UK and European practice, and 0.25 kPa in some metric tables.

Say you're sizing a low-pressure LPG system to a 0.5" w.c. drop. You grab a table from a supplier's catalogue that looks right, but it was built on a 1.0" w.c. drop. Its capacities are noticeably higher, so you pick a smaller pipe. It looks fine on paper, and the appliance at the end of the run is short of gas.

The fix is dull: read the table header every time. It should state the gas type, inlet pressure, pressure drop and specific gravity. If any of those don't match your job, you're on the wrong table.

| System type | Typical supply pressure | Typical allowable drop | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Residential NG, low pressure** | 7" w.c. | 0.5" w.c. | Most common US residential setup |
| **Residential LPG, after 2nd stage** | 11" w.c. (~300 mm w.c.) | 0.5" w.c. | Use LPG tables only |
| **LPG intermediate (1st to 2nd stage)** | 10 psig (max 20 psig) | Often 1 to 2 psi | Size with intermediate-pressure tables |
| **Elevated pressure NG (2 psi system)** | 2 psi | 1.0 psi | Needs a line regulator before appliances |
| **UK/EU domestic NG** | ~21 mbar at meter | ~1 mbar meter to appliance | Check BS 6891 tables |
| **Underground PE service lines** | Varies by utility or LPG supplier | Per design table | PE tables differ from steel |

### Pipe sizing workflows: longest length vs. branch length
NFPA 54 and the International Fuel Gas Code both accept two ways to size a gas network. The first is simpler. The second saves pipe. We'll use the same house for both so you can see where they differ.

The example job runs on natural gas at 7" w.c. supply with a 0.5" w.c. drop, in Schedule 40 steel.

| Appliance | Input | Distance from meter |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Furnace** | 100,000 BTU/hr | 18 ft |
| **Range** | 65,000 BTU/hr | 30 ft |
| **Tankless water heater** | 199,000 BTU/hr | 40 ft (furthest) |
| **Total** | 364,000 BTU/hr | (~364 CFH at 1,000 BTU/ft³) |

A main header leaves the meter. At 12 ft there's a tee to the furnace. The header continues to a second tee at 25 ft, where the range branches off, and carries on to the tankless heater at 40 ft.

### The longest length method
This is the quickest way to a safe answer.
1. Add up the connected load from the nameplate input of every appliance (input, not output). Here that's 364,000 BTU/hr, or about 364 CFH.
2. Measure from the meter (or the second-stage regulator on LPG) to the furthest appliance. Here that's 40 ft.
3. Size every section from that one length. Go to the 40 ft column of your table and size each section for the load it carries.

In a standard 0.5" w.c. natural gas table for Schedule 40 steel, the 40 ft column gives roughly 81 CFH for 1/2", 170 CFH for 3/4", 320 CFH for 1" and 657 CFH for 1-1/4".

| Section | Load carried | Size picked (40 ft column) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Meter to first tee** | 364 CFH | 1-1/4" (1" only carries 320) |
| **First tee to second tee** | 264 CFH | 1" |
| **Second tee to tankless** | 199 CFH | 1" (3/4" only carries 170) |
| **Branch to range** | 65 CFH | 1/2" |
| **Branch to furnace** | 100 CFH | 3/4" (1/2" only carries 81) |

It's conservative and quick. The cost is that short branches near the meter get sized as if they were 40 ft long.

### The branch length method
This method gets you smaller pipe on short branches without taking risks on the main run.
1. Size the main run to the furthest appliance exactly as before. Every section on the path from the meter to the tankless heater still uses the 40 ft column.
2. Size each branch using its total length from the meter to that appliance, not the length of the branch alone.

The furnace sits 18 ft from the meter, so round up to the 20 ft column. There, 1/2" carries about 118 CFH against a 100 CFH load, so 1/2" works. The range, at 30 ft, gets about 95 CFH from 1/2" against a 65 CFH load and stays at 1/2".

| Section | Longest length result | Branch length result |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Meter to first tee** | 1-1/4" | 1-1/4" |
| **First tee to second tee** | 1" | 1" |
| **Second tee to tankless** | 1" | 1" |
| **Branch to range** | 1/2" | 1/2" |
| **Branch to furnace** | 3/4" | 1/2" |

In a small house that's one branch. In a restaurant kitchen with eight burners and a fryer bank off one header, the savings in pipe, fittings and labour add up quickly.

Run the same house on propane at 11" w.c. with a 0.5" w.c. drop and the first main section drops from 1-1/4" to 1", because LPG carries about 2.5 times the energy per cubic foot. Using a natural gas table on an LPG job, or the other way round, gives you the wrong size.

## Fitting resistance and friction losses
Straight pipe isn't the only thing that costs you pressure. Every elbow, tee and valve adds turbulence, and turbulence eats pressure.

### The 5-foot rule vs. real equivalent length tables
Plenty of fitters were taught to add 5 feet of pipe for every fitting. It's easy to remember and wrong at both ends of the size range.

A 1/2" elbow behaves like about 1.5 ft of straight pipe, so adding 5 ft triples the real loss and you oversize the run. A 4" tee taken through the branch behaves like about 20 ft of straight pipe, so adding 5 ft understates the loss by a factor of four and the header ends up undersized. The second mistake is the dangerous one, because commercial kitchens and boiler rooms are where loads are highest and margins are tightest.

Before you add anything, check the table notes. Many capacity tables, including the NFPA 54 and IFGC ones, already allow for a normal number of fittings. If yours does, only add equivalent length for layouts with a lot of extra fittings.

| Nominal size | 45° elbow | 90° elbow | Tee (through branch) | Gate valve (open) | Flat rule |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1/2"** | 0.8 | 1.6 | 3.1 | 0.4 | 5.0 |
| **3/4"** | 1.1 | 2.1 | 4.1 | 0.4 | 5.0 |
| **1"** | 1.4 | 2.6 | 5.2 | 0.6 | 5.0 |
| **1-1/4"** | 1.8 | 3.5 | 6.9 | 0.7 | 5.0 |
| **1-1/2"** | 2.1 | 4.0 | 8.0 | 0.9 | 5.0 |
| **2"** | 2.7 | 5.2 | 10.3 | 1.1 | 5.0 |
| **3"** | 4.0 | 7.7 | 15.3 | 1.6 | 5.0 |
| **4"** | 5.3 | 10.1 | 20.2 | 2.2 | 5.0 |

### CSST fitting corrections
Corrugated stainless steel tubing (CSST) has its own rules, because the corrugations add friction along the whole length.

Most CSST manufacturers' sizing tables already allow for a set number of fittings and bends. For runs with more than that, several design guides use L = 1.3n, where n is the number of extra fittings or tight bends beyond what the table assumes, and L is the extra length in feet to add to the run. A run with 4 extra bends gets 1.3 × 4 = 5.2 ft added to its measured length before you go to the table.

Use the sizing table for the exact CSST brand you're installing. Different manufacturers' tubing has different internal profiles, and their tables aren't interchangeable.

## Material selection and underground pipe assembly
### Aboveground systems: Schedule 40 steel, copper and CSST
Schedule 40 steel (black iron) is still the default for exposed and commercial work. It's tough and takes threaded or welded joints, and no inspector will question it.

Many codes allow copper tubing for gas, with two checks first. Some local codes restrict it, and natural gas with a high sulphur content can attack copper over time. Make sure the fittings are rated for gas. Lead and lead-alloy pipe isn't allowed for gas at all, so if you find any in an old building, it comes out.

CSST is popular because it goes in fast with fewer joints. Size it by Equivalent Hydraulic Diameter (EHD) rather than the nominal size on the jacket: a higher EHD means more flow, and two tubes that look the same from different brands can carry different amounts of gas. Run it through a protective sleeve wherever it passes through masonry or concrete, never cast straight into a wall or slab. Fit striker plates at studs and joists where a nail or screw could hit it.

Gas piping has to be bonded to the building's electrical earthing system, and in lightning-prone areas a missed bond can damage CSST. In UK practice, main protective bonding conductors are typically 10 mm² copper. In the US, CSST bonding is commonly done with 6 AWG copper under NFPA 54. Attach the bonding conductor to a rigid steel section, a brass CSST fitting or a manufacturer-approved bonding point. A clamp on the corrugated tubing can damage it and won't give a reliable connection, and the code prohibits it.

### Underground medium-density polyethene (MDPE)
MDPE is the standard choice for buried runs. It doesn't rust and it copes with ground movement. It also comes in long coils, so there are fewer joints underground.

You'll see two main grades. PE80 covers general low and medium pressure distribution. PE100 gives higher pressure ratings, or thinner walls at the same pressure.

Gas PE pipe is usually yellow or yellow-striped so it's easy to spot when someone digs. It's for underground use only, because sunlight degrades polyethylene, and it must change to metal pipe through an approved riser before it comes above ground.

Mechanical stab fittings (Con-Stab style, for example) go together quickly, but each step is there for a reason:
1. Cut the pipe square with a proper PE pipe cutter, not a hacksaw. An angled cut won't seat evenly on the internal seal.
2. Chamfer the outside edge so the pipe slides past the O-ring without rolling or cutting it, then clean off any shavings.
3. Measure the insertion depth from the fitting (most have a depth gauge or a printed dimension) and mark it on the pipe with a paint pen.
4. Push the pipe in straight until your mark sits within about 1/8" of the fitting face. If the mark is still well outside, the pipe isn't fully engaged and the joint can pull out under pressure.

## Testing, purging and retrofitting existing gas lines
### Pressure testing and tightness criteria
A pressure test proves two things: the joints hold under pressure (strength), and the system doesn't leak at normal pressure (tightness).

NFPA 54 calls for a test pressure of at least 1.5 times the maximum working pressure, and never less than 3 psig. Many LPG installation specifications set higher multipliers for different parts of the system, for example:

| System section | Example test pressure |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Cylinder manifold / high-pressure section** | 1.5 × working pressure (e.g. 150 psig) |
| **Intermediate pressure lines (1st to 2nd stage)** | 2.5 × working pressure |
| **Low-pressure domestic lines** | 5 × operating pressure, often around 5 psig |

On home installations, the low-pressure line from the second-stage regulator to the kitchen is the section most likely to get tested in a hurry. Give it the same care as the high-pressure side.

These numbers vary by country, code and project spec, and your local code and the design spec decide the final figure. In India, that means PESO requirements and the relevant part of IS 6044, the code of practice for LPG installations.

People sometimes assume a tightness test proves zero leakage. It sets a maximum allowable pressure drop over a fixed time, which corresponds to a very small leak rate. In one commonly cited set of figures, a 4 mbar drop over 2 minutes works out to about 19 mL of gas per minute. For comparison, people can smell odorised gas at around 1 mL per minute, and the smallest leak that can build an ignitable mix in a normal room is estimated at around 14 mL per minute. So the limit sits close to the danger line, and a 5 mbar drop is a fail, not a near pass.

Use a gauge that can read small changes at your test pressure. A 0 to 300 psi dial gauge won't show a 4 mbar drop.

### Safe purging and regulator lock-up checks
After a pneumatic test the line is full of air, and it has to come out before anyone lights an appliance.
1. Purge through a temporary purge point that vents outdoors, away from ignition sources, doors, windows and drains (LPG sinks, so drains matter). On larger pipe, many codes require purging outdoors rather than into the room.
2. Displace the air with fuel gas until gas comes out of the purge point.
3. Confirm with a gas detector or a controlled test flame at a safe test burner. A steady flame that doesn't lift or go out means the air is gone.
4. Never purge into an appliance combustion chamber.
5. Close the purge point and leak-check it.

Lock-up is the pressure the regulator holds when no gas is flowing. To check it, fit a manometer downstream of the regulator, run an appliance to confirm working pressure, then shut the appliance off and watch the reading settle.

The pressure will rise a little when flow stops, which is normal. If lock-up settles more than about 20% above the regulator's set pressure, or keeps creeping up, the seat is probably worn or dirty. Replace the regulator before handover, because a creeping regulator can push appliances above their rated inlet pressure overnight.

### Capacity audits: adding high-BTU equipment to old lines
A lot of today's callbacks start here. A homeowner swaps a 40,000 BTU/hr tank water heater for a 199,000 BTU/hr tankless unit and expects the existing 1/2" line to cope.

It usually can't. On natural gas at a 0.5" w.c. drop, a 1/2" steel line carries about 172 CFH at 10 ft and only about 81 CFH at 40 ft. A 199,000 BTU/hr heater needs about 199 CFH on its own.

Before you quote, run this audit:
1. List every appliance on the system with its nameplate input, including anything the customer plans to add, such as a pool heater, outdoor kitchen or generator.
2. Map the existing pipe with sizes and measured lengths from the meter or regulator.
3. Check the meter and regulator capacity. The pipe can be fine while the meter is too small, and utilities and LPG suppliers can upgrade both.
4. Re-run the sizing for the new total load using the correct table for the gas and pressure.
5. Find the bottleneck. Often you only need to replace the first section from the meter plus the new appliance branch, not the whole house.
6. Fire every appliance at once and measure inlet pressure at the furthest one. It should stay within the manufacturer's stated range.

### Safety callout
* Never use pure oxygen for pressure or leak testing. Oxygen meeting oil or grease inside fittings can ignite violently. Use air, nitrogen or another inert gas.
* Never use a flame to look for leaks. Use leak detection fluid or an electronic detector.
* LPG sinks. Check pits, basements and drains before any ignition near an LPG purge or leak.
* Check lock-up on every new or replaced regulator.

### Tightness testing flowchart
1. Isolate the section and cap all open ends.
2. Pressurise to the required test pressure with air or nitrogen.
3. Let the pressure stabilise for the specified time.
4. Record the starting reading.
5. Hold for the specified test period.
6. Did the pressure drop exceed the allowed limit? If yes, find the leak with leak detection fluid, repair it and go back to step 2. If no, record the result, release the test pressure and move on to purging.

## Conclusion
Good gas sizing comes from using the right table for the job and refusing the shortcuts that look harmless. Know your gas and your pressure drop, measure runs properly and count fittings honestly. Then test and purge as if the inspector were standing behind you, and the 7 a.m. "no hot water" call gets a lot rarer.
